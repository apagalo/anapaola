Are Our Resources Following Our Priorities?

Using operational data to uncover resource trade-offs, challenge assumptions and turn insights into decisions across a 70+ project portfolio.

Strategy & Operations Data Analysis Resource Allocation Decision Support
TL;DR →

Context

I lead Design Operations at Electrolux Design for Product Line Wellbeing and SDA across Stockholm and Shanghai, with 70+ projects running across the portfolio.

Design capacity is finite, and designers split their time across different types of work: bringing products to market, developing brand and design identity, exploring future innovation, and supporting initiatives outside our immediate portfolio.

That makes one question particularly important:

Are we actually spending our time where we say our priorities are?

To answer it, I use recurring resource, portfolio and budget reporting to look beyond individual projects and understand how capacity is behaving as a system.

The Signal

Innovation had fallen from ~30% to ~5%.

One of the reports tracks how Design capacity is distributed across different types of work. Over time, the allocation had shifted significantly.

Type of workPreviouslyMore recently
Implementation~60%~70%
Innovation~30%~5%
Identity~10%~25%

The numbers alone weren't enough to say whether this was good or bad.

Identity had increased during a rebranding, so part of that shift made sense. Implementation was also the immediate business priority.

But Innovation falling to ~5% raised a different question, particularly for categories where innovation is part of the intended brand proposition:

Are we intentionally prioritizing today's delivery — or becoming reactive at the expense of tomorrow?

The Diagnosis

I wanted to understand the why before recommending the what.

I spoke with the designer leading Innovation to validate what I was seeing in the resource data.

His own research and observations supported the pattern: Innovation activity had reduced substantially, while immediate delivery requirements were absorbing more of the team's capacity.

That helped frame the issue differently. The question wasn't whether Innovation or Implementation should win. The real question was:

What level of Innovation capacity do we deliberately want to protect while Implementation remains the priority?

The Recommendation

I recommended making that trade-off explicit rather than allowing short-term demand to determine the allocation by default.

That meant starting a conversation with Product Line leadership — particularly around Vacuum Cleaners — to understand the intended role of Innovation and what future-focused work we wanted to protect.

The Innovation Design lead began developing a proposal for new Innovation work, aligned with the Head of Wellbeing & SDA Design.

The Decision

After discussing the trade-off with Design leadership, the decision was to keep Implementation as the priority while allocating 50% of the Innovation Design lead's capacity back to Innovation.

It wasn't a decision to choose the future over today's delivery.

It was a decision to protect part of the future while still delivering today.

A Second Signal

Small commitments were adding up.

The same reporting surfaced another resource question. Several designers were spending relatively small amounts of time supporting projects outside our core portfolio.

Individually, those commitments looked minor. Aggregated across the team, they represented meaningful capacity.

Rather than immediately recommending that we stop supporting those projects, I proposed understanding the demand first. I aggregated the hours being spent, forecasted future demand based on typical behavior, and investigated what type of Design support those projects actually required.

The question became:

How can we keep supporting the wider organization without allowing informal commitments to quietly consume our capacity?

The Recommendation

I recommended moving from ad-hoc support toward a clearer operating mechanism. I aligned with the Project Lead and we established a more formal way for projects to request and receive support from our designers, creating clearer expectations around the commitment required.

The goal wasn't to stop collaboration. It was to make the trade-off visible, forecastable and intentional.

What I Took From It

The number is rarely the interesting part.

Innovation at 5% is a number. Understanding what we might sacrifice by keeping it there is a strategic question.

A few hours spent on another project is a number. Seeing those hours accumulate across a team and changing how that capacity is committed is an operating question.

A budget forecast is a number. Seeing where we're heading early enough to respond turns it into a decision tool.

The part of data work I care about most is finding the question behind the number, forming a point of view, and turning it into something people can act on.
