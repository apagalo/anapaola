Redesigning Design Workflows for Continuous Improvement

As Electrolux Group's design organization scaled to 30 designers managing 50+ concurrent projects, existing workflows hadn't evolved to match. Processes that worked when teams were smaller began to break down — creating handoff gaps, unclear ownership, and inconsistent ways of working across 5 design leads and multiple portfolios.

Situation

Electrolux Group's design teams work on products that touch millions of people globally. Behind every product launch is a design process that involves concept development, user research, prototyping, validation, and production handoff — often with stakeholders spanning engineering, marketing, product management, and manufacturing across multiple countries.

Over time, these workflows had grown organically. Each team and region had adapted processes to their local context, which made sense individually but created friction at the organizational level. When a project required collaboration across portfolios — Wellbeing and Brand Expression, for example — teams would discover that their assumptions about timelines, deliverables, and approval gates didn't align. The design organization needed its processes to catch up with its ambition.

Challenge

The challenge wasn't that processes didn't exist — it was that they were inconsistent, undocumented, and not designed for scale. Specific pain points included:

  • Handoff friction: Transitions between design phases and between design and other functions lacked clear protocols, leading to rework and misunderstandings
  • Knowledge silos: Learnings from one team or project rarely made it to others, so the same problems were solved repeatedly in different parts of the organization
  • Tool fragmentation: Teams used different platforms and documentation standards, making it difficult to collaborate or find information across groups
  • Invisible bottlenecks: Without a systematic view of how work actually flowed, it was hard to identify where processes were breaking down

Approach

I addressed this through a combination of process mapping, collaborative redesign, and platform consolidation — always grounding changes in how teams actually work rather than imposing theoretical frameworks.

Process Mapping and Bottleneck Identification

Before redesigning anything, I mapped the actual workflows — not the intended ones, but how work really moved through the organization. I documented project flows across the Wellbeing, SDA, and Brand Expression portfolios, identifying where delays clustered, where handoffs broke down, and where teams spent time on work that didn't add value. This diagnostic work was essential for building credibility and ensuring that changes targeted real problems.

Workshop Facilitation

I designed and facilitated workshops with cross-functional teams to co-create improved workflows. These workshops served multiple purposes: they surfaced pain points from the people closest to the work, they generated solutions that teams felt ownership over, and they built alignment across groups that rarely had structured time to collaborate on how they work together. I also facilitated design thinking workshops focused on innovation and entrepreneurship, helping teams apply structured creative methods to their own operational challenges.

Platform Consolidation (SharePoint)

I built and maintained a centralized SharePoint platform as the operational hub for the design organization. This consolidated project documentation, process guides, templates, and reference materials into a single, searchable location. The goal was to make it easy to find the right information and hard to accidentally work from outdated documents.

Continuous Improvement Loops

Rather than treating workflow redesign as a one-time project, I established regular feedback loops. I gathered input from teams on what was working and what wasn't, reviewed process metrics during operational reviews, and made incremental adjustments. This approach meant workflows stayed relevant as the organization evolved, rather than becoming outdated documentation that nobody followed.

My Role

I led the end-to-end workflow improvement effort — from initial diagnostics through redesign, implementation, and ongoing iteration. I was the facilitator, the process designer, and the platform owner. I worked hands-on with individual teams to understand their pain points and with leadership to align process improvements with strategic priorities. I also coordinated the iconography team, ensuring consistent design standards across products — another area where clear process and coordination directly improved output quality.

Impact

  • Reduced handoff friction between design phases and cross-functional teams by introducing clear protocols and standardized documentation
  • Increased knowledge sharing across portfolios through centralized platform and consistent process documentation
  • Improved team alignment through structured workshops that brought distributed teams together around shared workflows
  • Established a continuous improvement cadence, ensuring processes evolved alongside the organization rather than becoming outdated
  • Elevated the design function's visibility by making operational improvements tangible and measurable for leadership

Key Takeaway

Process improvement in a creative organization requires a light touch and a collaborative approach. The biggest mistake is designing workflows in isolation and handing them to teams — the people doing the work are the ones who understand where it breaks down. The role of design operations is to facilitate that understanding, provide structure and tools, and create the conditions for teams to continuously improve how they work together.
